1. Summary

  1. The first run's over-merge numbers were mostly an Affinity effect, not a graph effect. 41.6% of matched nodes map to two or more Affinity people (shared devices, household emails). Once only single-person nodes count as labels, the share of matched components that are over-merged drops from 47.1% to 5.1%, and singletons can no longer be over-merged.
  2. The labels are now self-consistent. Tune holds 70% of people and holdout 30%, so they see different raw over-merge rates. After correcting for that, both say the same thing: about 38% of judgeable 2-node components and 63% of all judgeable components hold two or more Affinity people.
  3. 38% for a 2-node pair is too high to be random error. It most likely reflects household linking. Whether a component should be a person or a household is the main open decision: it changes how much of this counts as error.
  4. Size is the strongest signal. The corrected over-merge rate rises from about 38% at 2 nodes to about 97% above 128 nodes. The giant component (697M nodes) holds 91–95% of all over-merged IDs.
  5. Group size matters little once component size is fixed. The only sign of an effect is tapad in small components (5–8 nodes: 31% → 46% as tapad's largest group goes from 3 to 7–8). In 9–16-node components it's mixed, and from 17 nodes up no source shows any effect. liid and id5 are flat throughout. A tighter tapad cap (about 8) is worth testing, but the expected gain is modest.
  6. TTD, ID5_UID and ST don't make components worse at a given size. They grow components (and bridge into the giant), rather than adding bad links themselves.
  7. The graph splits people far more than it merges them: 91% of people with 2+ nodes are spread over several components, and 89% of components are singletons, mostly nodes whose groups were dropped by the cap.
  8. The models in notebook B add little beyond component size (over-merge AUC 0.52–0.67 within size bands). The descriptive tables in notebook A are the useful output.

2. What was measured

Affinity (the AFS crosswalk) is used only as a judge: it labels and evaluates components but never decides how any component is built or split. People are split by a hash of their AFS id into tune (70%) and holdout (30%); each split only sees its own people.

Each component gets a class from its single-person nodes (nodes that map to exactly one AFS person):

TermMeaning
class 0No node in the component maps to exactly one AFS person in this split (no label).
class 1Its single-person nodes all map to the same AFS person.
class 2 = over-mergedIts single-person nodes map to two or more AFS people.
judgeableHas at least 2 single-person nodes, so class 2 was possible.
incompleteA person in the component also has nodes in another component.
% over-mergedclass 2 ÷ (class 1 + class 2). Depends on how many labelled nodes a component has.
% over-merged (judgeable)class 2 ÷ judgeable. The fairer comparison across groups; used for most comparisons below.
correctedJudgeable rate adjusted for split size, i.e. what it would be if every person were visible (see below).

Split-size correction. If two nodes belong to the same person, both are visible in a split with probability s (the split's share of people); if they belong to two different people, with probability s². So the observed odds of over-merging are the true odds × s. Dividing the observed odds by 0.7 (tune) or 0.3 (holdout) gives the corrected rate. The two splits agreeing after correction is a good check that the labels behave as expected.

Components were sampled by a fixed hash of the component id (15%). The Affinity matching and each person's spread across components are always computed over the whole graph, so "incomplete" and "people split" are exact even in the sample. The giant component is in the sample.

3. The label fix: multi-person Affinity nodes

In the first run, any node that Affinity maps to two or more people made its component class 2. 44% of matched singletons were class 2 for that reason alone, which is impossible as a graph error since a singleton has no links. From the second run on, those nodes are counted but not used as labels.

  • id5: flat or slightly falling (9–16 nodes: 50.3 → 44.8; 17–32: 60.0 → 52.8).

Implication: once component size is fixed, the size of the largest linking group adds little. The one hint is tapad in small components, so a tighter tapad cap (about 8) is worth testing, but don't expect a large improvement from it. liid and id5 show no penalty from larger groups up to 16, so tightening them would fragment people further for no measurable gain. Component size itself is the strong signal, which points at limiting how components grow (the giant) rather than at per-source group caps. Groups above 16 aren't in this graph, so this says nothing about loosening.

One caution in reading these: in small components a large group nearly is the component, so group size and component make-up are hard to separate there.

Correction to an earlier reading: on the "% of matched" base, liid components looked much cleaner than tapad (6.5% vs 16.9% at 9–16 nodes). On the judgeable base the sources are similar at a fixed size (about 48–54% at 9–16 nodes). The gap was mostly detection: liid components hold more TTD/ST nodes, so fewer of them have two labelled nodes.

9. Fragmentation: incomplete components and split people

  • 89% of components are singletons (362.8M of 409.3M). Most are nodes whose every group was dropped by the cap of 16, typically at group sizes 17–128 in tapad and liid.
  • 91% of people with 2+ single-person nodes in the graph are spread over several components.
  • % incomplete (a person in the component also appears elsewhere) is 70–79% for components up to 64 nodes, and 83% for matched singletons. MAID singletons are 95% incomplete; HEM singletons 75%.

The graph's main failure mode for most people is under-merging, not over-merging. Tightening caps everywhere would make this worse; that's why the cap evidence (tapad only) matters.

10. The giant component

One component holds 697,176,027 nodes, 14.2% of all 4.91B nodes in IDGraph5, including 20.2M matched nodes (41.5% of them multi-person). It is over-merged under every label definition and accounts for 91–95% of all over-merged IDs. Its make-up: TTD 35.9%, ID5_UID 30.1%, HEM 11.1%, AAID 10.5%, IDFA 8.4%, ST 4.1%. Going from caps 32/10 to 16/6 shrank it from 47% of nodes to 14%, at the cost of the singleton share rising from 25% to 49%, so further global tightening is a poor trade.

Options under consideration: (1) re-split only the giant's edges with stricter rules (e.g. a tapad cap of 6–8, or keeping only edges two sources agree on) and re-run connected components on it alone; (2) per-source caps in a full rebuild; (3) treat it as "unresolved" downstream; (4) community detection inside it. Option 1 is the recommended next step. Since size, not group size, is what predicts over-merging, a rule that stops components growing past a size limit (e.g. two-source agreement for edges inside the giant) is more promising than a tapad cap alone.

11. Notebook B: the models

B predicts, for every component, the probability that it's over-merged and that it's incomplete, using only graph features. It trains on tune labels and is evaluated on holdout labels. Four models: lookup (rate of the component's cell), lr (logistic regression on size, source and type shares, group sizes, singleton reasons), and two naive-Bayes variants over (source × ID type) tokens (nb_sum, nb_mean).

12. Caveats

  • Person vs household. Affinity's unit is an individual. If the graph is meant to link households, a large share of "over-merged" is correct behaviour. The 38% rate on 2-node pairs is the clearest sign.
  • Detection bias. Over-merging can only be seen in components with 2+ single-person nodes. Groups with many unlabelled types (TTD, ID5_UID, ST) look cleaner on the "% of matched" base than they are; comparisons here use the judgeable base to reduce this.
  • The split correction assumes the tune/holdout assignment is independent of how people are linked. Tune and holdout agreeing after correction supports it.
  • Association only. None of the source or type comparisons are causal. They say where errors concentrate, not what causes them.
  • 15% sample. Large enough for every row shown (smallest cells reported have ≥200 judgeable components); rows with fewer are blanked. The giant is included.
  • One graph. All results are for IDGraph5 (cap 16). Groups above 16 don't exist in it.

13. Decisions and next steps

  1. Decide person vs household as the target. This changes how the over-merge numbers should be read.
  2. Split the giant with a stricter rule applied only to its edges (two-source agreement and/or a tapad cap of about 8), then score the pieces with notebook A.
  3. Optionally test a tapad cap of 8 in a full rebuild, keeping liid and id5 at 16, and compare with the same tables. Expect a modest effect.
  4. Run the full (100%) job only once a configuration is chosen; per-component scores need every component.
